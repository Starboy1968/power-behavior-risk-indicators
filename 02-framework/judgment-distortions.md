# How Judgment Gets Distorted (and What to Do About It)

Risk assessment is a judgment, even when it ends in a rating. Power and social pressure bend that judgment in predictable ways. This page is intentionally short: recognize the distortion, see how it shows up, apply a specific control.

| Distortion | How it shows up in a GRC setting | What you can observe | Debiasing control |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anchoring** | The first view offered (last year's rating, the vendor's self-assessment, the executive's opinion) sets the range for everyone else. | Final assessments sit close to the first view stated; prior-cycle text copied forward. | **Independent assessment before discussion:** each participant records a view in writing before the meeting. Hide prior-cycle conclusions until fresh views are in. |
| **Optimism** | Delivery teams expect fixes to land on time and controls to work as designed, especially when incentives reward "on track." | Remediation dates that keep slipping; "on track" status with no evidence; "should be fine." | **Pre-mortem:** "Imagine it's six months from now and this failed. Why?" Require evidence for every "on track." **Reference-class check:** how long did similar fixes actually take here? |
| **Groupthink** | A cohesive group values agreement over challenge, especially under time pressure or a strong leader. | Fast consensus; few questions; dissent absent from minutes. | **Rotating challenger role:** a named person each meeting whose job is to argue the other side. Record material dissent in minutes. |
| **Normalization of deviance** | A gap tolerated once becomes the norm because nothing bad happened. | Exceptions renewed again and again; "we've always done it this way." | **Exception expiry with re-approval at a higher level** on each renewal. Periodic look-back: "Which of our exceptions are now the real process?" |
| **HiPPO effect** (highest-paid person's opinion) | The most senior person's view carries the room regardless of evidence. | Senior speaks first; others converge; junior experts go quiet. | **Senior speaks last.** Collect written input first. Separate the expert briefing from the decision discussion. |
| **Availability** | Recent or vivid events feel more likely; quiet risks feel less likely. | Ratings jump after a news story and fall in quiet periods; "we've never had a problem." | **Reference-class data:** use the organization's own incident, near-miss, and audit history, plus external data where available, before judging likelihood. |

## Where incentives come in

Distortions get stronger when the person assessing a risk benefits from a particular answer: a bonus tied to go-live, a sponsor who wants good news, a team measured on "no overdue findings." The best control here is structural, not training:

- The person who benefits from accepting a risk should not be its only assessor or approver.
- Status definitions should be evidence-based ("green = tested and passed"), not self-declared.
- Second-line challenge should be documented, including when it changed the outcome.

## A note on numbers

This project deliberately avoids numeric scoring. Where an organization does use scores, the same distortions apply, and they are harder to spot because a number looks objective. The controls above work either way.
